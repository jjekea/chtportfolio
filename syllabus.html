Course Description

This course examines cultural heritage tourism and the history of American leisure, tourism, and mobility as a lens for practicing public history in the digital age. This course is premised on the belief that heritage and history does not need to be monumental to be meaningful – a local pool, family-owned restaurant, or a neighborhood bloc party can be just as important to the community as any listed monument on the National Register of Historic Places. Students will engage with scholarship on memory, visual culture, digital preservation, and cultural heritage management while developing critical literacy around the growing role of generative AI in cultural heritage practice.

Course Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

  1. 01Analyze the history of American leisure, tourism, and mobility by examining how race, class, gender, and regional identity have shaped who gets to participate in, and who gets excluded from, recreational and heritage spaces throughout U.S. history
  2. 02Evaluate how heritage sites are interpreted, managed, and contested at local, community, national, and international scales, critically assessing the frameworks and policies that determine whose history gets preserved and why
  3. 03Interpret personal and community archives, visual culture, and material ephemera as historical evidence, applying public history methodology to examine sources ranging from institutional records to family photographs and travel souvenirs
  4. 04Assess the role of emerging digital tools, including generative AI, in cultural heritage work, identifying the benefits, limitations, and ethical implications for research, interpretation, and representation
  5. 05Design and produce a collaborative digital exhibit that presents an argument for the heritage value of a personally significant tourism site or experience to a non-specialist public audience

AI Policy Statement

Overview

The practice of doing public history is fundamentally about how we interpret, represent, and make arguments about the past for public audiences. Today, AI tools are increasingly a part of that landscape. As in other fields however, museums, archives, and cultural heritage organizations, continue to reckon with how we can ethically integrate AI while remaining attentive to best practices in terms of research, interpretation, and representation. This course frames AI use is a similar fashion: not as something to prohibit or uncritically adopt, but as a set of tools worth learning to use thoughtfully and with a critical lens.

In this course, the use of AI tools is permitted, as well as encouraged in specific contexts. Students will have the opportunity decide for themselves their comfort level with incorporating AI tools into their practice, bearing in mind that these tools are just that – tools. AI use should not be viewed as a shortcut, but as an object of inquiry and practical experimentation as we learn about cultural heritage work in the current, digital age. Whether or not we choose to incorporate AI into our practice, understanding both the benefits and limitations is necessary as we evaluate what makes good work in this field and where the field is headed next.

Throughout the course, we will develop AI literacy skills through several assignments that build towards our final project, the Personal Heritage Tourism Digital Exhibit. Although you will not be required to use AI in your final project, you will be encouraged to experiment with AI tools as long as that process is undertaken intentionally, discussed with the instructor ahead of time, and fully disclosed with appropriate documentation in both your final digital exhibit and reflection essay.

Using AI in This Course

Appropriate Use

  • Transcribing documents
  • Translating non-English archival materials
  • Generating alt-text to accompany images for digital accessibility
  • Drafting and workshopping interpretive text
  • Designing and constructing your digital exhibit

Inappropriate Use

  • Using AI to generate your main argument, interpretive framework, or exhibit narrative
  • Using AI as a substitute for primary source research

As we work with AI in this class, it is important to acknowledge the broader limitations and ethical dimensions of these tools from the outset. AI models are trained on datasets that reflect existing inequalities in what has been preserved, digitized, and made accessible to the public. Your final projects will center around personal memories and heritage experiences and in documenting these lesser-known places, there is a higher chance that AI may produce unreliable or distorted results. This has direct implications for your research, especially when working with stories of underrepresented communities. The use of AI tools also raises important questions about labor and copyright. As a public historian with a background in cultural heritage preservation and digitization work at a federal institution, these are questions I continue to grapple with. As we learn together in this course, I encourage you to experiment, reflect, and consider not only if the AI output is useful but whose knowledge it draws on, and what, or who, it may be leaving out.

If you’re ever in doubt about what is or is not appropriate, ask! As with any developing technology, there is a grey area, and we can always discuss options for AI use with proper attribution and prior instructor approval. We will go into more detail about proper AI use and documentation later in the course.

In this course, students will be encouraged to learn and explore with the use of AI tools, but the ultimate decision to use AI will be your own. For students that don’t wish to use AI with their personal materials, alternate options will be made available, and no one will be penalized for failing to incorporate AI elements in their final project.

It is fully possible to do successful work in this class without AI, but understanding these tools is necessary as we evaluate the shape of the field going forward. Ultimately, AI is a tool that can help or hinder us, just as any other tool can. If you choose not to use AI, that decision is important too and worth reflecting on.

Contact the instructor with any questions.

Late Assignment Policy

All assignment due dates are posted in WebCourses and listed in the course schedule below. Unless otherwise noted, assignments are due by 11:59 PM Sunday night following our class.

Deadlines for Reading Response Papers are purposefully flexible to account for students’ time in weeks that other assignments are due, but all three must be submitted by the end of Week 13. For all group assignments (other than in-class activities), students will have the opportunity to revise and resubmit after initial instructor feedback.

If you are unable to submit any assignment by the stated deadline, contact me prior to 5:00 PM on Sunday to arrange an extension.
