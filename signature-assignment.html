Assignment Overview

For the final project in this course, students will research, design, and build a digital exhibit to document a personally significant tourism/leisure experience and make an argument for its value as heritage, why it matters, and why it should matter to others.

The heritage asset you choose does not need to be famous or even well-known to the general public. It does not need to be listed on the National Register of Historic Places or managed by the National Park Service. Does the site hold meaning for you personally? If so, you will be well on your way to successful completion of this project. Examples of potential choices could include: a summer camp, a beach your family returns to every year, a local baseball stadium, a neighborhood park, a regional festival, an old movie theatre that has since closed, and more. The less obvious your site is, the more original your contribution will be to this project.

Throughout the semester, students will work individually and in small groups as an exercise in collaborative public history practice. Students will research the history and management (or lack thereof) of their individual heritage asset, curate a collaborative, digital exhibit in groups of 3–4, and present their work to the class in the final week of the semester.

Learning Objectives

  1. 01Apply historical research methods and cultural heritage management concepts to curate a digital exhibit drawing on primary sources, visual culture, and personal and family archives.
  2. 02Evaluate the role of personal and community memory within broader historical narratives and articulate what is at stake in preserving these sites for the communities they serve.
  3. 03Collaborate with peers to produce a cohesive, scholarly, interpretive narrative that is accessible to a non-specialist public audience, and critically engage with public history work in practice.

Context for Delivery in the Course

In this course, we will examine the historiography of heritage tourism and cultural heritage preservation practice in the United States and internationally, with a focus on digital methods for archiving and storytelling (including the use of AI tools), visual culture, place identity, and memory.

Your project will build on these elements throughout the semester and consist of multiple scaffolding assignments that you will incorporate into the final, collaborative digital exhibit and presentation.

Component Assignments

Part 1 · Week 3 · 5%

Individual Topic Selection

Students will submit a topic selection proposal (1–2 pages) on their chosen heritage tourism asset, consisting of a brief overview of the site/experience and their connection to it, a map of the site, and 3–4 images with appropriate citation. These can be historical, publicly available and contemporary, or personal photographs. Students will provide a short description of each image and summarize their plans (or any unanswered questions) for the visual and creative component of the project.

Part 2 · Week 5 · 10%

Visual Analysis Exercise: In-Class Workshop, Digital Artifact Generation, Discussion Post

Students will attend an in-class workshop on AI tools and heritage interpretation in order to critically examine how generative tools can be used (and misused) in public history and cultural memory work.

In class, students will analyze a set of 8–12 family photos, working with Claude to reconstruct context, dates, and narrative, while reflecting on the capabilities and limitations of AI-assisted research.

The goal of the assignment is to get students thinking about constructing a cohesive, historical narrative based on their own family photos, ideally relating to their digital exhibit project. However, students that do not wish to upload their own photos to AI will be able to select an alternate set of archival photos, chosen prior to class from the American Folklife Center digital collections: loc.gov

Before the next class, students will be asked to take what they’ve learned from the activity and use Claude Artifacts to generate one interactive element as an initial prototype for their digital exhibit, and upload the artifact alongside a brief discussion post reflecting on the process of working with AI.

Part 3 · Week 6 · 5%

Annotated Bibliography

Students will submit an initial annotated bibliography consisting of at least five sources based on their preliminary research.

Part 4 · Week 8 · 15%

Group Proposal

In groups of 3–4 (as decided by the instructor by Week 5), students will submit a group proposal outlining the “big idea” for their digital exhibit – based on museum consultant Beverly Serrell’s approach to exhibit planning as discussed in Exhibit Labels: An Interpretive Approach (2015), which we will refer to throughout the semester. In this proposal, students will articulate the overarching interpretive narrative that frames their exhibit, summarize each group member’s site argument in relation to that narrative, outline how work will be divided, discuss plans for digital tools that will be used in the exhibit, and submit any unanswered questions or concerns about the work so far. Groups will revise this proposal and submit the updated exhibit narrative along with their final digital exhibit.

Part 5 · Week 13 · 40%

Group Digital Exhibit

Digital exhibits will be due in Week 13 for initial instructor feedback. Full assessment plan below.

Part 6 · Week 14 · 15%

Group Presentation

Each group will present their digital exhibit in the last week of class followed by five minutes of Q&A. Students will have the opportunity to resubmit their final digital exhibit following feedback from peers and the instructor if desired (due the following week).

Part 7 · Week 15 · 10%

Individual Reflection Essay

Students will submit a brief reflection essay (2–3 pages) documenting research decisions, the experience of working with digital tools, any use of AI-assisted elements, and a reflection on the collaborative process of doing public history work in practice.

Digital Exhibit Assessment Plan

Group Digital Exhibit — Heritage Tourism and Preserving Community Memory · worth 40% of the final grade.

Individual Site Argument15%

Each student will research their personally significant site and make the case for its value as heritage. This will include historical contextualization using primary and secondary sources, an analysis of current site management or lack thereof, and a clear argument for its preservation geared towards a general audience. Students will incorporate their research into the digital exhibit along with appropriate citation and submit a peer review form outlining their individual contribution.

Visual and Creative Component10%

Each student will evaluate and incorporate their own travel ephemera, photographs, or home videos as historical evidence for interpretation, supplemented with archival materials as needed (minimum 10 visual objects should be included with full citations and interpretive text). At least one creative element (such as repeat photography, a fictionalized diary entry, drawing, etc.) is required for each individual heritage asset. This component will allow students to incorporate AI, but that decision must be approved ahead of time and clearly labeled as such in the exhibit.

Team Exhibit Narrative & Collaborative Digital Exhibit15%

Students will develop an overarching narrative that connects their sites thematically, historically, or argumentatively to curate a unified digital exhibit. Students will submit an initial group proposal in Week 8 outlining the exhibit narrative and submit an updated document reflecting the final digital exhibit in Week 13. Successful digital exhibits will be visually cohesive, organized with a clear connecting narrative, proof-read and free from obvious grammar and spelling errors, and thoroughly cited. Students will be permitted to use AI tools in building their exhibit, but any AI use must be clearly documented in the final exhibit narrative and website.

Students will not be limited in the platforms they are permitted to use for their exhibits, but each tool has a different learning curve and different capabilities so it will be important to discuss this with the instructor ahead of time. AI Guidelines

AI tools are permitted in this project, but any usage must be clearly documented in the final digital exhibit and component assignments, intentional, and evaluated critically for appropriate use in historical research and design.

Allowed & Encouraged

  • Transcribing handwritten documents, degraded materials, or audio and video from personal and family archives
  • Translating non-English materials in family or community archives
  • Generating alt-text for exhibit visual materials to support digital accessibility
  • Drafting and workshopping interpretive text, with the understanding that all AI-assisted drafts must be substantially reviewed and revised so that the final voice, narrative, and argument is your own
  • Designing and constructing your digital exhibit (formatting, layout, built-in AI website builder tools on the platform itself)

Allowed With Prior Approval

  • AI-generated imagery as a creative element or digital artifact such as a map or timeline. Must be clearly labeled in the exhibit and your reflection essay must explain the decision for this choice
  • AI-assisted research must be verified against primary sources and critically evaluated for historical accuracy

Not Allowed

  • Using AI to generate your main argument, interpretive framework, or exhibit narrative. All AI-generated writing must be reviewed, revised, and clearly disclosed as AI-assisted.
  • Using AI as a substitute for archival, primary source research

Documentation

Your final digital exhibit and reflection essay must clearly denote any use of AI tools in this project, as you would cite any other source. If you didn’t use AI in this process, note that choice clearly and explain your rationale in the reflection essay. If you did use AI, include the following:

  • Which tools you used and for what tasks
  • At least one example of a prompt used, including screenshots and a description of how you evaluated the output and revised your approach
  • A critical evaluation on what AI did well, where it made mistakes, what biases you found, and what working with these tools tells us about the role of AI in public history practice
