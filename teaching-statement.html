Teaching Statement

A reflection on teaching philosophy and approach to public history pedagogy.

Author: Jordan Kearschner

When I first began graduate school in public history, I was certain I wanted to work in a museum – not a classroom. Over time however, I realized that this distinction was much more superficial than I had originally thought. Museums, historic sites, and university history departments all ask the same fundamental question: how do we help people make meaning from the past? In 2018, in the final year of my master’s program, I wrote a blog post for the International Federation for Public History reflecting on this sentiment. It was well-received at the time and later reposted on my university’s website, but after my graduation and subsequent employment in the cultural heritage field, that essay eventually slipped from my CV in favor of more “practical” work. Early in 2026 however, days into the second semester of my PhD program, I received an email from a public history professor in another state that unexpectedly brought this 2018 blog post back into my mind. I had not read my own words in years, but it seemed others had. This professor wrote to tell me that he was assigning my blog post in his public history course (for the second time!) and invited me to speak with his students. This was, to put it lightly, a shock. Until then, I had never considered that this piece of writing had remained in circulation, let alone become a part of someone else’s teaching. This experience reinforced what years of museum and digital preservation work have underscored: teaching history isn’t about where we teach it. Teaching history is about how we invite others to ask questions, evaluate evidence, and connect historical scholarship to the world around them.

Before returning to academia in 2025, I spent six years working in collections digitization at the Library of Congress where historians, archivists, educators, conservators, and technologists collaborate daily to preserve and interpret the cultural heritage of the United States. This experience continues to shape my approach to teaching. I view historical knowledge not as something to be transmitted directly from expert to student, but as something students acquire by creating, interpreting, and communicating for themselves. As a public historian, my goal is to help students see themselves not simply as repositories for historical facts and figures, but as emerging practitioners capable of contributing thoughtfully to conversations about the past.

Teaching history isn’t about where we teach it. Teaching history is about how we invite others to ask questions, evaluate evidence, and connect historical scholarship to the world around them.

Collaboration and participatory practice are thus central to my work as a public historian and educator. Public history is just that – public – and learning to work with other people is paramount to the work we do. Cultural heritage institutions depend on teams with diverse expertise and perspectives to present often complex historical narratives to diverse audiences that may hold very different beliefs from our own – especially in this current day and age. For emerging public historians, effective collaboration is an integral skill that we must develop in order to prepare for our professional work after graduation. As an educator, I believe in structuring these experiences intentionally, while providing opportunities for peer review and individual accountability, so that students can develop both historical knowledge and the collaborative habits that will carry them beyond the classroom.

With a background in digital preservation, my teaching is similarly informed by digital humanities and the power of digital tools to expand access to historical learning and cultural literacy. Increasingly, the public is encountering history through digital exhibits and archives, podcasts and video essays, and community-based storytelling projects rather than through scholarly work or visits to the museum. The debates and controversies around the place and legitimacy of history are taking place online as well. Digital humanities provides a framework for students to think critically about how historical knowledge is preserved and shared, while considering questions of accessibility, authority, and representation. Although not all students in a public history course will go on to work in the field, the ideas we engage with in class have practical implications for anyone that seeks to understand both the past and how we reconcile that history with the world we live in today – and where we can go from here.

Today’s students know better than most the ability of digital tools and online spaces to shape personal opinions and public discourse, but learning to use these tools in intentional ways, grounded in scholarship, requires a particular approach. As a late millennial, my experiences growing up and learning in the digital age are not so distant from students’ today. I would be remiss however to assume that I necessarily know how to better navigate this digital world than someone a decade younger than me. As a public historian and educator, my teaching philosophy therefore emphasizes that learning is a two-way street – collaboration and communication go both ways. As we work to design projects that can reach audiences beyond the classroom and scholarly circles, it is important to remember that we too are a part of that community. Learning to listen and work with one another – and learn from one another – are essential components of public history practice, especially as new digital tools emerge to complicate the ways in which we engage with one another.

In the last few years, generative AI has dramatically shifted the ways we interact online, bringing with it particular challenges to how we learn, teach, and interpret history for public audiences. But like many other developing technologies of the past, it is important to not ignore the opportunities, nor the ways in which our work must evolve to accommodate the new environment we find ourselves within. Rather than approaching AI as either a threat to academic integrity or the inevitable solution to every problem, I view it as an object of critical inquiry. In this course, I encourage students to experiment with AI for a variety of tasks including transcription, translation, and improving accessibility, while remaining attentive to the challenges of doing historical interpretation in a time when we cannot always trust the legitimacy of what we see online and in the public sphere. This issue is not new – we can look to the history of censorship and propaganda in this country as an obvious example – but we cannot ignore that the ways we receive and transmit information are changing every day.

AI is a tool like any other, but how we use and evaluate it determines the meaning we take away from it.

In order to combat AI-generated, mass-proliferated misinformation about history, we must understand it too. AI is a tool like any other, but how we use and evaluate it determines the meaning we take away from it. Rather than prohibit AI in my classes, I encourage students to explore, document if and when they incorporate it into their work, evaluate output critically, identify biases, and reflect on the ethical implications of using AI within cultural heritage work. Ultimately, I want students to leave the classroom with practical AI literacy skills, grounded in public history best practices, that will better prepare them to engage with the public in a professional capacity and navigate their place in today’s digital age.

In the 2018 blog post, I wrote “Even if visitors do not retain specific facts from their trip to a museum, ideally, they leave with a greater propensity to seek out new information and become more critical of the world around them in pursuit of the truth.” This has taken on new dimensions in the age of AI, as quickly evolving LLM models have made it all the more difficult to tell fact from fiction. The underlying premise is the same, however. By engaging with historical scholarship and seeking to understand the new technologies that are creating the world we live in today, we are better equipped to understand our place within it. Whether I am developing museum exhibits and programming or teaching in a university classroom, my philosophy remains the same. Teaching is an extension of public history itself. It is an opportunity to connect scholarship with broader audiences, cultivate curiosity through historical inquiry, and prepare learners to thoughtfully interpret the past. If students leave my classes more attentive to whose stories are being preserved, more confident in communicating those histories to others, and more willing to engage critically with both traditional and emerging forms of public history practice, I consider that a success.

— Jordan Kearschner
